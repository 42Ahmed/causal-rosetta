So you want to be a math wizard who can prove any estimator? This chapter serves as a mathematical summary for the book Introductory Ecconometrics. After finishing this chapter, you will be able to solve all math problems. 

1.1 Basic Math Tools

1.1.1 The Summation Operator and Descriptive Statistics

The summation operator (\(\sum\)) is essential for manipulating formulas involving datasets. Given a sequence of \(n\) numbers \(\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}\), the sum is written as \(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n\).

Key Summation Properties:

1. Sum of a constant: For any constant \(c\), \(\sum_{i=1}^n c = nc\). 

2. Factoring out a constant: \(\sum_{i=1}^n cx_i = c\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\). 

3. Sum of a linear combination: \(\sum_{i=1}^n (ax_i + by_i) = a\sum_{i=1}^n x_i + b\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\). 

Note of caution: The summation operator does not distribute over ratios or squares. The sum of ratios \(\sum (x_i / y_i)\) is not equal to the ratio of the sums \(\sum x_i / \sum y_i\), and \(\sum x_i^2 \neq (\sum x_i)^2\).

Descriptive Statistics and Averages:

Sample Average (Mean): \(\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\).

Sum of Deviations: If you subtract the mean from each observation to get a deviation \(d_i = x_i - \bar{x}\), the sum of these deviations is exactly zero: \(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0\).

Sum of Squared Deviations: A critical algebraic fact for upcoming OLS proofs is that the sum of squared deviations can be rewritten: \(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2 - n(\bar{x})^2\).

Cross-Products: For two variables \(x\) and \(y\), the sum of their cross-deviations behaves similarly: \(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i(y_i - \bar{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})y_i\).

1.1.2 Properties of Linear Functions

A linear function takes the form \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\), where \(\beta_0\) is the intercept and \(\beta_1\) is the slope.

Marginal Effect: The defining feature of a linear function is that the marginal effect of \(x\) on \(y\) is constant. The change in \(y\) is given by \(\Delta y = \beta_1 \Delta x\).

Multiple Variables: For \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2\), the change in \(y\) is \(\Delta y = \beta_1 \Delta x_1 + \beta_2 \Delta x_2\). If \(x_2\) is held fixed (\(\Delta x_2 = 0\)), then \(\Delta y = \beta_1 \Delta x_1\). This is the foundation of the ceteris paribus (holding other things equal) concept in multiple regression.

1.1.3 Proportions and Percentages

You must strictly distinguish between a percentage change and a percentage point change.

Percentage change: Evaluates the change relative to the initial value: \(\%\Delta x = 100 \cdot \frac{x_1 - x_0}{x_0}\).

Percentage point change: If a variable is already measured as a percentage (e.g., an unemployment rate drops from 8% to 6%), the difference is a change of \(-2\) percentage points, not \(-2\) percent (it is actually a \(25\%\) decrease).

1.1.4 Some Special Functions and Their Properties

To model relationships where marginal effects are not constant, econometricians rely on specific non-linear functions.

1.1.4.1 Quadratic Functions

Used to capture increasing or diminishing marginal returns.

Equation: \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2\).

Turning Point: The function reaches a maximum (if \(\beta_2 < 0\)) or a minimum (if \(\beta_2 > 0\)) at \(x^* = \frac{\beta_1}{-2\beta_2}\).

Marginal Effect: The effect of \(x\) on \(y\) changes based on the starting value of \(x\). Approximated by \(\Delta y \approx (\beta_1 + 2\beta_2 x)\Delta x\).

1.1.4.2 The Natural Logarithm

The natural log (denoted as \(\log(x)\) or \(\ln(x)\)) restricts inputs to \(x > 0\) and is widely used because it allows us to approximate percentage changes directly.

Algebraic Properties: \(\log(x_1 x_2) = \log(x_1) + \log(x_2)\) \(\log(x_1 / x_2) = \log(x_1) - \log(x_2)\) \(\log(x^c) = c \log(x)\).

Percentage Approximation: For small changes, the change in the log of a variable represents its proportional change: \(\Delta \log(x) \approx \frac{\Delta x}{x}\). Multiplying by 100 gives the percentage change: \(100 \cdot \Delta \log(x) \approx \%\Delta x\).

Constant Elasticity Model: If \(\log(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(x)\), then \(\beta_1\) represents the elasticity of \(y\) with respect to \(x\) (the \(\% \Delta y\) for a 1% increase in \(x\)).

Log-Level (Semi-Elasticity) Model: If \(\log(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\), then a 1-unit increase in \(x\) leads to a \((100 \cdot \beta_1)\%\) change in \(y\).

1.1.4.3 The Exponential Function

The exponential function, \(y = \exp(x)\) or \(e^x\), is the inverse of the natural logarithm, meaning \(\log(\exp(x)) = x\) and \(\exp(\log(x)) = x\).

Properties: \(\exp(x_1 + x_2) = \exp(x_1)\exp(x_2)\).

Use in Econometrics: It is used to transform a log-linear equation back to its original levels. If \(\log(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\), then \(y = \exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x)\).

1.1.5 Differential Calculus

Calculus formalizes the “marginal effect” approximations using derivatives.

Ordinary Derivatives: The derivative \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) evaluates the slope of a curve at a specific point. For small changes, \(\Delta y \approx \frac{dy}{dx} \Delta x\).

If \(y = x^c\), then \(\frac{dy}{dx} = cx^{c-1}\).

If \(y = \log(x)\), then \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{x}\).

If \(y = \exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x)\), then \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \beta_1 \exp(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x)\).

Partial Derivatives: For multivariate functions like \(y = f(x_1, x_2)\), the partial derivative \(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_1}\) measures the slope in the direction of \(x_1\), treating \(x_2\) as a constant. The ceteris paribus effect is calculated as \(\Delta y \approx \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_1} \Delta x_1\).

First Order Conditions (Optimization): To find the minimum or maximum of a function \(f(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k)\), you take the partial derivatives with respect to every variable \(x_j\) and set them all to zero: \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j} = 0\) for all \(j=1, 2, \dots, k\). This is exactly how the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimators are derived.

1.2 Probability

1.2.1 Random Variables and Their Probability Distributions

A random variable takes on numerical values determined by an experiment (a procedure that can be infinitely repeated with well-defined outcomes). We denote random variables with uppercase letters (e.g., \(X\)) and their particular outcomes with lowercase letters (e.g., \(x\)).

1.2.1.1 Discrete Random Variables

A discrete random variable takes on a finite or countably infinite number of values.

Bernoulli (Binary) Random Variable: The simplest type, taking only values of 1 (success) or 0 (failure). If \(X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\theta)\), then \(P(X=1) = \theta\) and \(P(X=0) = 1-\theta\).

Probability Density Function (PDF): Summarizes the possible outcomes and their probabilities. For a discrete variable, \(f(x_j) = P(X=x_j) = p_j\), where \(\sum p_j = 1\).

1.2.1.2 Continuous Random Variables

A continuous random variable takes on any real value with a probability of exactly zero. Because probabilities of specific values are zero, we focus on ranges of values.

Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF): Defined as \(F(x) = P(X \le x)\), it evaluates the probability that \(X\) is less than or equal to a number \(x\). It is always between 0 and 1, and is a nondecreasing function.

Calculating Probabilities: The probability that \(X\) falls between \(a\) and \(b\) is computed via the CDF: \(P(a \le X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)\).

1.2.2 Joint Distributions, Conditional Distributions, and Independence

1.2.2.1 Joint Distributions and Independence

When dealing with multiple random variables, their relationship is described by a joint PDF, \(f_{X,Y}(x,y) = P(X=x, Y=y)\).

Independence: Random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if their joint PDF is the product of their marginal PDFs: \(f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x)f_Y(y)\). Knowing the outcome of \(X\) tells us nothing about the outcome of \(Y\).

Binomial Distribution: The sum of \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials (each with success probability \(\theta\)) follows a Binomial distribution: \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta)\). The probability of exactly \(x\) successes is \(\binom{n}{x} \theta^x (1-\theta)^{n-x}\).

1.2.2.2 Conditional Distributions

The conditional PDF of \(Y\) given \(X=x\) is \(f_{Y|X}(y|x) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)}\). It evaluates \(P(Y=y | X=x)\). If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, then \(f_{Y|X}(y|x) = f_Y(y)\).

1.2.3 Features of Probability Distributions

1.2.3.1 The Expected Value and its Properties

The expected value (or population mean), \(E(X)\) or \(\mu\), is the probability-weighted average of all possible values of \(X\). For a discrete variable, \(E(X) = \sum x_j f(x_j)\). E(X) does not necessarily correspond to a value that \(X\) can actually take. Key Properties:

1. Constants: \(E(c) = c\).

2. Linearity: \(E(aX+b) = aE(X) + b\).

3. Sums: The expected value of a sum is the sum of expected values: \(E(\sum a_i X_i) = \sum a_i E(X_i)\).

Note: For nonlinear functions, \(E[g(X)] \neq g(E[X])\) in general (e.g., \(E(X^2) \neq [E(X)]^2\)).

1.2.3.2 The Median

The median is the value \(m\) where half the area of the PDF is to the left and half to the right. If a distribution is symmetric about its mean \(\mu\), then the mean and the median are equal.

1.2.3.3 Variance and Standard Deviation

Variance measures the expected squared distance of \(X\) from its mean: \(Var(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2] = E(X^2) - \mu^2\). Variance is always non-negative, and denoted \(\sigma^2\). Key Properties:

1. Constants: \(Var(c) = 0\).

2. Linear Combinations: \(Var(aX+b) = a^2 Var(X)\) (Adding a constant does not change variance; multiplying scales it by the constant squared).

Standard Deviation (\(sd(X)\) or \(\sigma\)) is the positive square root of the variance. It restores the measure of dispersion to the original units of the variable. Property: \(sd(aX+b) = |a|sd(X)\).

1.2.3.4 Standardizing, Skewness, and Kurtosis

  • Standardization: To create a variable \(Z\) with a mean of 0 and a variance of 1, subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation: \(Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}\).
  • Skewness: Measured by the third moment, \(E(Z^3)\). If a distribution is symmetric, skewness is zero.
  • Kurtosis: Measured by the fourth moment, \(E(Z^4)\). It captures the “thickness” of the tails. The standard normal distribution has a kurtosis of 3.

1.2.4 Features of Joint and Conditional Distributions

1.2.4.1 Covariance and Correlation

Covariance measures the linear dependence between two variables: \(Cov(X,Y) = E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)] = E(XY) - \mu_X\mu_Y\).

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, \(Cov(X,Y) = 0\) (but zero covariance does not strictly imply independence).

\(Cov(a_1 X + b_1, a_2 Y + b_2) = a_1 a_2 Cov(X,Y)\).

Correlation Coefficient (\(\rho_{XY}\)) standardizes covariance to remove the influence of measurement units: \(Corr(X,Y) = \frac{Cov(X,Y)}{sd(X)sd(Y)}\).

Bounded: \(-1 \le Corr(X,Y) \le 1\).

\(Corr(X,Y) = 1\) implies a perfect positive linear relationship; \(-1\) implies a perfect negative linear relationship.

1.2.4.2 Variance of Sums of Random Variables

The variance of a sum accounts for covariance: \(Var(aX + bY) = a^2 Var(X) + b^2 Var(Y) + 2ab Cov(X,Y)\). If \(X\) and \(Y\) are uncorrelated (\(Cov=0\)), the variance of the sum (or difference) is the sum of the variances: \(Var(X \pm Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)\). For \(n\) pairwise uncorrelated variables: \(Var(\sum a_i X_i) = \sum a_i^2 Var(X_i)\).

1.2.4.3 Conditional Expectation

The conditional expectation \(E(Y|X=x)\) tells us how the average value of \(Y\) varies with specific outcomes of \(X\). In econometrics, this is frequently modeled as a function, like \(E(Y|X) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X\). Key Properties:

CE.1: \(E[c(X)|X] = c(X)\) (Functions of \(X\) act as constants when conditioning on \(X\)).

CE.2: \(E[a(X)Y + b(X)|X] = a(X)E(Y|X) + b(X)\).

CE.3: If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, \(E(Y|X) = E(Y)\).

CE.4 (Law of Iterated Expectations): \(E[E(Y|X)] = E(Y)\). The unconditional expectation of \(Y\) is the expected value of the conditional expectation.

CE.5: If \(E(Y|X) = E(Y)\), then \(Cov(X,Y) = 0\) (but zero covariance does not guarantee \(E(Y|X)=E(Y)\)).

1.2.4.4 Conditional Variance

The conditional variance is \(Var(Y|X=x) = E(Y^2|x) - [E(Y|x)]^2\). If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, the conditional variance equals the unconditional variance: \(Var(Y|X) = Var(Y)\).

1.2.5 The Normal and Related Distributions

1.2.5.1 The Normal Distribution

The normal distribution is characterized by its bell shape, defined purely by its mean (\(\mu\)) and variance (\(\sigma^2\)): \(X \sim Normal(\mu, \sigma^2)\).

Standard Normal Distribution: \(Z \sim Normal(0,1)\). The CDF of \(Z\) is denoted \(\Phi(z)\).

Standardizing: Any normal variable can be standardized to compute probabilities: \(P(X \le x) = P\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma} \le \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right) = P(Z \le z) = \Phi(z)\).

1.2.5.2 Additional Properties of the Normal Distribution

  • A linear transformation of a normal variable remains normal: \(aX+b \sim Normal(a\mu+b, a^2\sigma^2)\).
  • If two jointly normal variables are uncorrelated, they are absolutely independent.
  • Any linear combination of independent normal variables is normally distributed. For example, the sample average \(\bar{Y} \sim Normal(\mu, \sigma^2/n)\).

1.2.5.3 The Chi-Square Distribution

If you square and sum \(n\) independent standard normal variables, \(Z_i \sim Normal(0,1)\), the resulting variable \(X = \sum Z_i^2\) has a chi-square distribution with \(n\) degrees of freedom (\(X \sim \chi^2_n\)). The mean is \(n\) and the variance is \(2n\).

1.2.5.4 The t Distribution

The t distribution is formed by dividing a standard normal variable \(Z\) by the square root of an independent chi-square variable \(X\) (divided by its degrees of freedom \(n\)): \(T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{X/n}} \sim t_n\). The t distribution has thicker tails than the normal, but approaches the standard normal distribution as the degrees of freedom (\(n\)) approach infinity.

1.2.5.5 The F Distribution

The F distribution is the ratio of two independent chi-square variables, each divided by their respective degrees of freedom: \(F = \frac{X_1/k_1}{X_2/k_2} \sim F_{k_1, k_2}\). \(k_1\) is the numerator degrees of freedom, and \(k_2\) is the denominator degrees of freedom.
